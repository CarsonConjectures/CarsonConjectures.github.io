Read the space.

The corner is the joint. Its height is the joint density f(x, y), or probability mass for a discrete table. The two arms keep the layout of a contingency table.

The first ridge on each arm is a marginal. Integrating over Y gives f(x); integrating over X gives f(y). Discrete tables use sums.

The remaining ridges are conditionals. A violet slice extends the X axis at a fixed Y coordinate and shows f(x | y). An amber slice extends the Y axis at a fixed X coordinate and shows f(y | x). The slices run perpendicular to their denominator marginal: f(y) for f(x | y), and f(x) for f(y | x). Each defined slice integrates (or sums) to 1.

∞ Continuous joins all conditioning values into two surfaces. Along an arm, the predicted variable varies. Across an arm, the conditioning value stays aligned with the same row or column of the joint. This is a finite-resolution rendering of a continuous family.

Scale matters. “Fit each family” makes the shapes easy to see; heights across families are not comparable. Choose “Shared density scale” to compare numerical heights. The chart normalizes over your selected domain, so changing its bounds changes the distribution.

Drag to orbit; scroll or pinch to zoom. Focus the chart and use ← → ↑ ↓, + / −, or R to reset. Hover over a surface or ridge to inspect its value.

Density expression reference

Numbers, x, y, pi, e, + - * / ^, comparisons and && || are supported. Functions: exp log sqrt abs sin cos tan pow min max. Use explicit multiplication, e.g. 2*x. Comparisons evaluate to 0 or 1: (x > 0) * exp(-x-y) * (y > 0). Only finite, nonnegative densities are accepted.

Continuous inputs use trapezoidal quadrature on a 65 × 65 grid with linear interpolation between samples. Very narrow peaks need a tighter domain. A zero marginal makes that conditional undefined; gaps are left in the plot.
